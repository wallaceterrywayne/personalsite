The problem

Students in many health science programs, especially in nursing and pharmacy programs, often struggle with dosage calculations and related problems. While many methods exist (desired/have, proportional reasoning, dimensional analysis), dimensional analysis is often suggested as the most powerful and recommended by these programs. And, while dimensional analysis is by far the most robust method, many students still struggle with using it correctly. Students often struggle with identifying their target in setting up dimensional analysis problems, identifying irrelevant information, parsing the problem itself, and losing track of units. I developed the WHACC method to attempt to mitigate these issues.

The WHACC Method

The WHACC acronym stands for

  • Want,
  • Have,
  • Align,
  • Cancel, and
  • Calculate.

We will explain the method as we look at an example problem of the sort that might be commonly seen in a nursing school program.

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

In the WHACC method, the first step is the Want step. Here, the student is asked to identify and write down the units the problem wants. In this case, it wants milliliters.

NoteWHACC: Want

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

The next step asks the student to identify all information they Have to work with. Importantly, we do not ask the student to identify relevant or irrelevant information. This step is only about getting numbers and units out of the problem to reduce cognitive load.

NoteWHACC: Have

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

With the Want and the Haves written down, the student is asked to begin the Align step. As dimensional analysis is commonly taught, no correct first term is typically required. For the WHACC method, the student is asked to align one of their Haves with a unit in the Want. In this case, the Want consists of only one unit, mL, so the student will align mL. They represent the Want with an unknown magnitude, typically x, and then begin building the standard dimensional analysis equation consisting of a chain of products of fractions. We also clarify that a unit “by itself” should be treated as being on top of a fraction. The student will then Align the relevant item in their Haves with the unit or units in the Want. The student’s Haves only contain a single instance of mL, 0.25 mg/mL, so the student must begin there.

NoteWHACC: Align

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

Align: x \text{ mL} = \frac{1 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \text{ mg}}

Now, the student can begin canceling unwanted units. The student is encouraged to ask themselves after every step “Do I have what I want yet?” or “Do my units match?”.

NoteWHACC: Cancel

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

Cancel: x \text{ mL} = \frac{1 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \cancel{\text{ mg}}} \times \frac{0.5 \cancel{\text{ mg}}}{1 \text{ day}}

Now, the student has eliminated the unwanted units of milligrams, and introduced unwanted units, days. From this point, the student will engage in the standard dimensional analysis algorithm, repeatedly switching back and forth between aligning and canceling units until both sides of the equation have matching units. They should continue to ask themselves “Do I have what I want?” after every step. That is the key to knowing when the algorithm terminates and to knowing what Haves are irrelevant.

NoteWHACC: Align & Cancel

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

Align & Cancel: x \text{ mL} = \frac{1 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \cancel{\text{ mg}}} \times \frac{0.5 \cancel{\text{ mg}}}{1 \cancel{\text{ day}}} \times \frac{5 \cancel{\text{ days}}}{1}

Now, if the student is diligently checking their units after each step, they will notice that their units now match. Milliliters are the only units on both the left and right hand side of the equation. This is the signal to stop adding information. A student might want to use the 50 \text{ kg}, but they can be redirected by asking “why” or by noting that they already have what they Want, so the kilograms do not help. Once their units match, they move on.

NoteWHACC: Calculate

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

Calculate: x \text{ mL} = \frac{1 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \cancel{\text{ mg}}} \times \frac{0.5 \cancel{\text{ mg}}}{1 \cancel{\text{ day}}} \times \frac{5 \cancel{\text{ days}}}{1} = \frac{1 \times 0.5 \times 5 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \times 1 \times 1}

For the calculate step, the student is encouraged to rewrite the right hand side of the equation as a single combined fraction before doing any calculation. This is to 1) give the student an opportunity to check their units, 2) reduce cognitive load by reducing a messy expression with lots of cancellations and marks, 3) make the expression safer and easier to enter into a calculator in a stressful, timed environment like an exam. Only then is the student allowed to enter anything into a calculator.

NoteWHACC: Calculate

An order says to administer digoxin 0.5 mg IV daily. The drug concentration available from the pharmacy is digoxin 0.25 mg/mL, and the patient weighs 50 kg. How many mL will be administered over 5 days?

Want: \text{ mL}

Have: \frac{0.5 \text{ mg}}{\text{day}}, \frac{0.25 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 50 \text{ kg}, 5 \text{ days}

Calculate: x \text{ mL} = \frac{1 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \cancel{\text{ mg}}} \times \frac{0.5 \cancel{\text{ mg}}}{1 \cancel{\text{ day}}} \times \frac{5 \cancel{\text{ days}}}{1} = \frac{1 \times 0.5 \times 5 \text{ mL}}{0.25 \times 1 \times 1} = 10 \text{ mL}

In the WHACC method, the student is discouraged from using a calculator at any point outside of the explicit Calculate step. Unit conversions, if they are necessary, can be folded into the standard align and calculate steps. This is to prevent “juggling” separate calculations and potentially losing track of units or what operations have or have not been done. This rule also reduces cognitive load in stressful exam conditions. The act of switching between writing on paper or a whiteboard to entering things into a calculator requires a student to change their focus, put down pencils or markers, and other little instances of cognitive load. For many students, this is magnified by their exam software, which requires them to use only a calculator in the exam software itself (for example, Examplify will provide a Desmos practice calculator to students for common nursing and pharmacy school exams). Since this computer-based calculator tends to be unfamiliar to them, switching back and forth would require even more load. To minimize this, we minimize the number of times a student has to switch by requiring that they save all calculations for the very end, after they have confirmed their units match.

Design rationale

The WHACC method was designed to address common problem I observed when working with students on these sorts of problems. The most common issues encountered were

  • not knowing when to stop/including irrelevant information,
  • not knowing how to begin, and
  • losing track of calculations.

Beginning each problem by identifying the Want, as well as the constant repetition of “Do I have what I want yet?” after each step in the Align and Cancel cycle, are meant to keep the student focused on the units and give them a clear sign for when they can move on. By beginning the very first Align step with, not an arbitrary choice of starting point but a careful selection of a factor with a desired unit, the student is locked into a solution process that (most of the time) produces the correct answer.

The Want and the Have steps also give the student a relatively simple task to start with while breaking down the problem from a potentially dense paragraph to a simple list of relevant information. This allows the student to immediately put pen to paper without having to worry about parsing the paragraph itself in one go. By requiring the student to write down all numbers and units in the Have step, they can also save any worrying about relevant vs. irrelevant information for the dimensional analysis step itself, where that sort of determination often falls out naturally by just paying attention to the units.

The final calculate step also forces the student to avoid juggling calculations throughout the problem. It’s very easy to drop or repeat steps by accident when the student is doing the calculations piecemeal, and this is a common source of “silly” mistake. In settings like nursing school, where no partial credit is given, we want to make the process as smooth as possible, with as many guiderails as possible. Saving all the calculations for the end, after the units can all be cancelled out and compared to the desired units, gives a powerful tool for students to catch these sorts of silly errors. Likewise, only having a single calculator use reduces the potential for transcription errors to a minimum.

I’ve also found this method to be very useful in reducing cognitive load. For many problems, the solution can be found by a rote application of the WHACC method, and the student never has to make a single active decision. The Want and Have steps reduce the amount of rereading the student has to do from the problem paragraph. Starting with a unit that matches a unit the student Wants gives a clear starting point for dimensional analysis, and the Calculate step minimizes the number of awkward switches between pen and paper (or marker and whiteboard) and calculator, which can be useful for stressed, frustrated, or distracted students during exams.

Reflection

I am very happy with the WHACC method, and I’ve found it to be very useful in getting students comfortable with dimensional analysis. Dimensional analysis as applied to these sorts of problem often stops being a math problem and becomes more of a jigsaw puzzle, where the student is merely flipping fractions one way or another to line up the units in the appropriate way. Using the WHACC method, I’ve consistently seen students shift from trying to work out a long and complicated math problem to a straightforward spatial reasoning approach, where their only concern is whether or not they want a particular unit on top of the fraction or on bottom.

That being said, the WHACC method still occasionally requires some active judgement from students. In particular, problems where there are repeated units among the Haves might require a student to select which repetition is the relevant one. For example, we might consider the problem

The doctor orders a patient be given 60 mg of Drug C every 4 hours. The package insert for Drug C gives a range of 5-15 mg/kg/day. Drug C is available in a concentration of 10mg/ml, and the patient’s weight is 44 pounds. Is this a safe dosage?

If we follow through on the WHACC method, we inevitably wind up with

NoteWHACC: Align

The doctor orders a patient be given 60 mg of Drug C every 4 hours. The package insert for Drug C gives a range of 5-15 mg/kg/day. Drug C is available in a concentration of 10mg/ml, and the patient’s weight is 44 pounds. Is this a safe dosage?

Want: \frac{\text{mg}}{\text{dose}}

Have: \frac{60 \text{ mg}}{\text{dose}}, \frac{1 \text{ dose}}{4 \text{ hr}}, \frac{5\text{–}15 \text{ mg}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{day}}, \frac{10 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, 44 \text{ lb}

Align: x \ \frac{\text{mg}}{\text{dose}} = \ ?

Here, the student has to choose between \frac{60 \text{ mg}}{4 \text{ hr}}, \frac{5\text{–}15 \text{ mg}}{\text{kg} \cdot \text{day}}, and \frac{10 \text{ mg}}{\text{mL}}, and the WHACC method requires some additional rules to make that clear. (In this case, I normally teach the student to decide between the package label and the order by looking at the language used by the question. Phrases like “will be administered”, “to be infused”, “to be given” refer to what you are ordered to do, so you start with the order. “Safe dosage” on the other hand, refers to general guidelines, which you get from the package insert.)
