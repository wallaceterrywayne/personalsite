The problem

It can be difficult to learn a new technology, particularly one involved with finicky technical work such as statistics. AI, when properly used, can be immensely valuable in helping a student learn a new technology, and is able smooth the learning process out, helping to break down syntax, decode error messages, and identify coding bugs. This particular project was born from a self-study strategy I used personally to get a handle on the statistical language R, as well as adapting my workflow to more appropiately address the needs of students.

For students learning a new language, we want a workflow that enables students to learn independently, while keeping the responsibility for learning firmly on the student’s shoulders.

Design rationale

For new students learning game design and attempting to create their own games, a common adage is “finish a game as quickly as possible”. It’s important for a new learner to see all stages of game design to gain a better understanding of game design as a holistic process. When learning a new statistical langauge and learning how to perform a statistical analysis, it is useful to use a similar approach, and this trainer is an attempt to build a workflow to get a student producing a minimum viable statistical analysis in their language of choice as quickly as possible and then checking the quality of that analysis against a vetted reference analysis.

Following principles of good design, we divide the workflow into two distinct stages, a planning stage and a reveal stage. Each stage begins with a new AI instance and has its own dedicated AI orientation documents and student briefs. The planning stage AI recieves its orientation document, the experimental design description, and the dataset. The reveal stage AI receives its own orientation document, the experimental design description, the dataset, as well as the solution key and the student’s statistical report. This way each stage is kept independent, and the planning AI is unable to easily give away any information to the student. This was a common problem I encountered in my initial experiments with this workflow: an AI would be instructed not to reveal any information about the intended analysis, and then do something like name the dataset “logistic-regression-data.csv”.

While initial experimentation had the AI generate the datasets and the experimental design descriptions on the spot (and it did surprisingly well at this), we found that this had the potential for subtle errors. For example, one analysis examined generated data about patient recovery after different types of heart surgeries. In this scenario, the variable length_of_stay measured the number of days post-surgery the patient stayed at the hospital, and this variable was intended to be correlated to the type of surgery. When the data was generated, however, this correlation was not implemented, which lead to a difference between the intended analysis and the actual data. Since these sorts of errors are hidden in sets of data, they can be difficult to spot, and so we avoid this problem by pre-generating and testing experimental design descriptions and datasets to ensure accuracy.

Each pregenerated dataset comes with a chance of having a potential complication in the data that the student must account for. The current list of possible complications is violations of assumptions, underpowered true effects, aliased variables, outcome-dependent variables, post-treatment variables, confounding variables, and unbalanced groups. This is an intential design choice to force the student to diligently check their assumptions and variables. Currently, zero to one complications are chosen when generating each dataset, and these complications are checked to make sure they measurably distort the outcomes of the analysis if not correctly handled with the exception of underpowered true effects. For underpowered true effects, these complications are not meant to distort the analysis, but to impress on the student that even a correct analysis can miss true effects due to random chance in sampling or underpowered experiments.

TipClaude · turn 19

Your list covers the core assumptions. A few additions and one clarification:

Your list:

  1. Independence of observations, which you argue from the design.
  2. Linearity between each continuous covariate and the outcome.
  3. Homogeneity of regression slopes: yes, you test this with interaction terms.
  4. Normality of residuals.
  5. Homogeneity of variance, which concerns the variance of the outcome residuals across groups. The table you made shows SDs of age and BMI, which are covariates, so it doesn’t speak to this assumption. You’ll still want to check it separately.

Commonly added:

  1. Covariates independent of the treatment. Often argued from randomization, and you can point to your balance table.
  2. Outliers and influential points, which aren’t strictly an assumption but are part of a standard check.
  3. Multicollinearity among predictors, if you have several covariates. vif() from car checks this.

How to check each one in R. Replace the right-hand side with whatever model you choose:

fit <- lm(qor15_30d ~ approach + covariate1 + covariate2, data = dat)

fit_int <- lm(qor15_30d ~ approach * covariate1 + approach * covariate2, data = dat)
Anova(fit_int, type = 3)        # look at the interaction rows
anova(fit, fit_int)

ggscatter(dat, x = "covariate1", y = "qor15_30d", color = "approach",
          add = "reg.line")

res <- residuals(fit)
ggqqplot(res)
shapiro.test(res)

leveneTest(res ~ approach, data = dat)

vif(fit)

check_model(fit)

For the slopes check, a non-significant interaction is consistent with equal slopes. For Levene’s test and Shapiro-Wilk, a non-significant p-value means no evidence of a violation. With n = 200, Shapiro-Wilk can flag trivial departures, so many people lean on the Q-Q plot as the primary evidence.

TipClaude · turn 21

Your instinct is right on both counts, and this is a well-studied question.

What randomization actually guarantees

Randomization makes covariates independent of treatment in expectation, over all possible randomizations. Any single realized allocation can be imbalanced by chance. The key point is that this chance imbalance is already accounted for in the inference: p-values and confidence intervals are calibrated over the randomization distribution, which includes the unlucky allocations. So “we had some imbalance by chance, and we proceed” is the textbook-correct stance, not biting a bullet.

Why significance-testing balance is discouraged

Many trials used to report p-values comparing baseline characteristics between arms. CONSORT and most methodologists now advise against this. If randomization was done properly, the null hypothesis is true by construction, so any “significant” difference is by definition a false positive. A descriptive table (means, SDs, percentages) is the recommended way to report balance. Then you judge whether any difference is large enough to matter clinically, not statistically.

How imbalance is handled in practice

  • Pre-specified covariate adjustment. This is the main tool, and it’s what ANCOVA does. Adjusting for prognostic baseline covariates corrects for chance imbalance in them and usually tightens the confidence interval. The important part is choosing covariates in advance, based on prior knowledge of what predicts the outcome, not after looking at which ones came out imbalanced. Choosing adjustment variables based on observed imbalance opens the door to analytic flexibility.
  • Design-stage fixes, done before allocation:
    • Stratified (blocked) randomization: randomize separately within strata, e.g., diabetic and non-diabetic patients, so those groups are balanced by design.
    • Minimization: each new patient is allocated, partly at random, to whichever arm best preserves balance across several factors. It’s common in smaller trials.
    • Rerandomization: your “hacky” idea is actually a legitimate method (Morgan & Rubin, 2012), if the acceptance rule is specified in advance (e.g., “redraw until the Mahalanobis distance between arms is below threshold a”), and the analysis accounts for the restricted set of allowable allocations. What’s hacky is doing it ad hoc and then analyzing as if it were simple randomization.

Summary: randomize once (or with a pre-specified restriction), report balance descriptively, adjust for pre-specified prognostic covariates, and don’t let the observed balance drive the analysis choices.

Partial eta-squared: a code fix and a definition

The fix: notice the header says “Type I”. eta_squared() on an lm object uses sequential sums of squares by default, so those values don’t match your Type II table. Pass it the Type II table instead:

eta_squared(Anova(fit, type = 2), partial = TRUE, alternative = "two.sided")

alternative = "two.sided" also gives a regular two-sided CI. The default one-sided CI with the upper bound fixed at 1.00 is why every row showed 1.00.

The definition: your phrasing is close. Precisely,

\eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{effect}}}{SS_{\text{effect}} + SS_{\text{residual}}}

In words, it’s the proportion of variance attributable to that term, out of the variance not explained by the other terms in the model. For approach, about 7% of the variance remaining after age, BMI, and diabetes is attributable to approach.

You can verify it by hand from your table: 2141.0 / (2141.0 + 27930.6) \approx 0.071.
